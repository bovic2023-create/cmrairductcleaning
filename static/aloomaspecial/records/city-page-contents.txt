That percentage varies significantly: a well-installed and recently maintained duct system may leak at 10 percent. The Duct Blaster Test: Putting a Number on Leakage<h2>
A duct blaster is a calibrated fan assembly that attaches to the air handler cabinet, with all registers and supply and return grilles temporarily sealed. The fan pressurizes the duct system to 25 Pascals above ambient atmospheric pressure. At that pressure differential, any opening in the duct system allows air to escape. The fan flow rate required to maintain 25 Pascals is the system's total leakage, measured in CFM25 (cubic feet per minute at 25 Pascals of pressure).
Normalizing that number against the home's conditioned floor area gives a leakage rate in CFM25 per 100 square feet. Most energy efficiency programs and building codes target below 8 to 10 CFM25 per 100 square feet for existing residential duct systems. A home with 2,000 square feet of conditioned space and a leakage measurement of 180 CFM25 has a leakage rate of 9 CFM25 per 100 square feet, which is within range. The same home with a leakage measurement of 400 CFM25 has a leakage rate of 20 CFM25 per 100 square feet, which is approximately 200 percent of the target.
The second test is performed after sealing to document the reduction. A home that tested at 20 CFM25 per 100 square feet before sealing and 7 CFM25 per 100 square feet after sealing has had its leakage rate cut to a third of its previous value. That difference is real, specific, and directly traceable to the energy and comfort gain the homeowner experiences after sealing.

The Three Sealing Methods and When Each One Applies<h2>
Not every duct leak is sealed the same way. The correct method depends on the leak's size, location, and accessibility.
<list>
Mastic sealant is a thick, paste-consistency polymer compound applied with a brush or putty knife to accessible duct joints, boot connections, and seams. It fills gaps and cracks up to 1/4 inch wide, conforms to irregular surfaces, remains flexible as the ductwork expands and contracts with temperature changes, and maintains its seal for up to 30 years when applied correctly. Mastic is the preferred sealing material for accessible connections in basements, crawlspaces, mechanical rooms, and attics where the technician can reach the joint directly. For gaps larger than 1/4 inch, fiberglass mesh tape applied first provides a substrate that mastic bonds to.^^
UL 181-rated foil tape is a pressure-sensitive metal-backed tape specifically tested for HVAC duct applications. It adheres to clean, smooth metal duct surfaces and is appropriate for flat seams on rigid sheet metal duct. UL 181 tape is not the same as the cloth-backed "duct tape" sold at hardware stores. Cloth duct tape fails quickly under the temperature cycling that duct systems experience and is not an acceptable long-term sealing material. We use UL 181-rated tape only on appropriate surfaces where mastic cannot be applied cleanly.^^
Aerosolized duct sealant addresses the leaks that cannot be reached from outside. Leaks in duct sections running through wall and ceiling cavities, inside enclosed chases, and in areas where demolition would be required to gain physical access to the duct surface cannot be sealed with mastic or tape without opening the structure. Aerosolized sealant pressurizes the duct system and injects polymer particles that travel through the duct interior. At points of leakage, the particles accumulate as they exit with the escaping air, eventually bridging and sealing the opening from the inside. The process can seal gaps up to 5/8 inch and reaches sections of ductwork that no other method can address without demolition.
After aerosolized sealing, a post-treatment duct blaster measurement documents the final leakage rate. The before-and-after comparison is a computer-generated report showing the starting leakage measurement and the ending leakage measurement, which is available to the homeowner as documentation of the work performed and the result achieved.

Where Duct Leaks Are Most Common in #State Homes<h2>
Understanding where leaks concentrate helps explain why duct leakage testing catches conditions that no visual inspection reliably finds.
<list>
Register boot connections are the points where the supply duct meets the ceiling or floor surface at each supply register. The boot is typically a sheet metal box that connects the round or rectangular duct to the register grille. The joint between the boot and the surrounding drywall or subfloor is one of the most common leak locations in residential duct systems. It is often sealed inadequately at installation and frequently disturbed when registers are painted or the surrounding surface is repaired.^^
Plenum takeoffs are the connections at the main supply trunk or air handler where individual branch ducts depart for each room. These connections are under pressure whenever the system runs. Original mastic or tape seals at these points dry, crack, and separate over time. A loosened plenum takeoff can produce a significant leak at a location that is difficult to inspect without entering the attic or mechanical space.^^
Mid-run joints occur wherever two sections of duct connect along the run. These joints are sealed at installation with mastic or tape that is subject to the same aging and thermal cycling as all other duct connections. In systems that have experienced any settling, pest activity, or attic disturbance, mid-run joints are common leak sources.

Dryer Vent Cleaning in #State: What Your Installation Is Required to Look Like<h2>
Here is a fact that surprises most #State homeowners: the dryer vent that has been running without obvious problems since the house was built may have been non-compliant with building code since the day it was installed.
Dryer vent installation is governed by International Residential Code Section M1502, and the requirements in that section are specific. The duct must be rigid smooth metal, minimum 4 inches in diameter. It must terminate outside the building, with a backdraft damper and no screen. It must not terminate in an attic, a crawlspace, a garage, or any interior space. It must not use plastic, vinyl, or ribbed foil duct for the main duct run. And the total equivalent length of the run, accounting for all bends, must not exceed the code maximum.
Many dryer vent systems installed by appliance delivery companies or general contractors over the past two decades do not meet all of these requirements. They work, in the sense that the dryer runs and the clothes get dry. They may also be accumulating lint faster than a correctly configured system, creating airflow restriction that shortens dryer life and increases fire risk, and terminating in a location that saturates building materials with moisture from the exhaust.
A non-compliant system that has not produced a visible problem is not a compliant system that is working fine. It is a non-compliant system that has not yet produced a visible problem.
$$

The IRC M1502 Requirements: What Your #State Dryer Vent Must Meet<h2>
Understanding what the code actually requires gives you a framework for evaluating your existing installation against a specific standard rather than a general sense of whether it seems okay.
<list>
Material requirements. The main duct run from the wall connection to the exterior termination must be rigid smooth metal with a smooth interior surface and a minimum wall thickness of 0.016 inches. Plastic duct is prohibited. Flexible foil duct is prohibited for the main run. Ribbed flexible metal duct is prohibited. The smooth interior requirement is not arbitrary: corrugated and ribbed duct surfaces create turbulence that reduces airflow velocity, and lint particles that lose velocity drop out of the airstream and accumulate in the corrugations. The accumulated lint is both a clog source and a fire fuel.
The transition section connecting the dryer to the wall entry point is separately specified. A transition duct (the short connection visible behind the dryer) may be semi-rigid metal or UL 2158A-listed flexible duct, maximum 8 feet in length, and must not be concealed inside walls or ceilings. The transition duct must be visible at all points.^^
Maximum equivalent run length. The IRC specifies a maximum equivalent run length for dryer exhaust ductwork. The base maximum is 35 feet from the connection to the transition duct to the exterior termination point. Each 90-degree bend in the duct run reduces the allowable length by 5 feet. Each 45-degree bend reduces it by 2.5 feet. A run with two 90-degree bends and one 45-degree bend has already consumed 12.5 feet of the 35-foot maximum in bends alone, leaving 22.5 feet for the straight duct run.
When the equivalent length exceeds the maximum, airflow velocity through the duct drops below what is needed to carry lint particles to the exterior. Those particles drop out of the airstream and accumulate inside the duct, progressively narrowing the effective cross-section.^^
Termination requirements. The duct must terminate at the building exterior. Termination in an attic is a code violation. Termination in a crawlspace is a code violation. Termination in a garage is a code violation. These prohibitions exist because dryer exhaust carries moisture, lint, and for gas dryers, combustion byproducts. Releasing these inside a structure creates moisture damage, fire risk, and in the case of gas dryers, potential CO exposure.
The exterior termination must be equipped with a backdraft damper: a spring-loaded or gravity-closed flap that opens when the dryer runs and closes when it stops, preventing outdoor air, pests, and precipitation from entering the duct when the dryer is off. Screens are prohibited at the exterior termination. Lint accumulates on screens within months of installation, eventually blocking the opening almost entirely. A nearly blocked termination creates back pressure that forces the dryer to work harder and heat up, accelerating wear and creating fire conditions.

Air Duct Cleaning in #State: What Happens to Flex Duct in a Hot Attic<h2>
Here is what most homeowners have never been told about their HVAC system's flex duct, even though it explains a significant portion of their summer utility bill.
In a typical #State summer, attic air temperature at the roof deck level reaches 130 to 150 degrees Fahrenheit during peak afternoon hours. Your supply ductwork runs through that attic, carrying air conditioned to 55 degrees from the air handler to the supply registers in your rooms. The duct is insulated, but insulation is not a barrier. It is a rate-limiting layer. Heat still transfers from the 140-degree attic air through the insulation and into the 55-degree conditioned airstream inside the duct.
A supply duct running 30 feet through a 140-degree attic does not deliver 55-degree air to the room. It delivers air that has absorbed heat through that entire run. The longer the run, the higher the attic temperature, and the poorer the condition of the outer insulation, the warmer the air arriving at the register. The system runs longer trying to overcome the heat gain that occurs within the duct itself.
This is before accounting for what accumulated debris does to the duct's internal performance. But understanding that attic duct cleaning is not the same operation as cleaning ducts inside conditioned walls is the foundation for understanding why attic duct systems require their own service approach.

The Flex Duct Conditions That Reduce Airflow in Your #State Home<h2>
Flex duct performance in unconditioned attics degrades through specific mechanical conditions, each of which reduces airflow to the rooms the duct serves.
<list>
Inner liner tension. Flex duct has three layers: an inner liner made of film-covered wire helix that holds the duct open, a fiberglass or polyester insulation layer in the middle, and an outer vapor barrier. The inner liner must be installed under tension: pulled as straight and tight as the run allows so that the wire helix maintains a smooth, open cross-section. A flex duct run that is loose and slack has its inner liner bunched into a corrugated shape that creates turbulence and adds significant airflow resistance at every corrugation. A correct installation of flex duct and a slack installation of the same duct in the same run can produce airflow differences of 30 to 50 percent at the register.
When we inspect flex duct in #State attics, one of the first things we assess is inner liner tension. A loose inner liner is a straightforward correction: the duct is detached at one end, pulled taut, and re-secured with the inner liner extended. This single correction often produces immediate, measurable improvement in airflow to the room being served.
Support spacing and kinking. Flex duct must be supported at regular intervals to prevent sagging. The correct support spacing is every three feet, using straps or hangers with a minimum width of three inches. Straps narrower than three inches create a pinch point that constricts the duct at the support location. Duct hanging from strapping at wider intervals develops sag between support points, and where that sag is significant, the inner liner loses tension and begins to kink at the low point. A 90-degree kink in a flex duct run can reduce airflow through that run by 50 percent or more.
Sagging duct sections also accumulate debris at the low point of the sag. Water vapor in the conditioned air can condense at the low point of a sag in certain conditions, creating a moisture accumulation that promotes biological growth inside the duct. Inspecting and correcting support spacing is a standard component of our attic duct service in #State.
Insulation degradation. The outer insulation layer on flex duct protects the conditioned air inside from the unconditioned attic environment. In #State attics, the combination of high summer temperatures and UV exposure at any section near roof penetrations or attic hatches degrades the outer vapor barrier over time. Once the vapor barrier is compromised, the fiberglass or polyester insulation becomes exposed to attic moisture. Wet insulation loses its thermal performance. A section of duct with degraded outer insulation is not just aesthetically damaged: it is a thermal bypass allowing unconditioned attic air to interact directly with the conditioned air inside.
Return duct infiltration. Return ducts bring air from the living spaces back to the air handler for reconditioning. When return ducts in an unconditioned attic have gaps or loose connections, the system draws attic air into the return stream rather than indoor air. Attic air at 140 degrees and with any accumulated particulates enters the air handler, is distributed through the supply side, and arrives in the living space. The system works harder to condition incoming air that is dramatically hotter than the setpoint differential it was designed to address. Return duct gaps also pull attic particulates, insulation fibers, and biological material from the attic environment into the supply air.

What Correct Structural Duct Repair Involves<h2>
A disconnected flex duct connection requires mechanical reconnection before any sealing can hold durably.
<list>
The splice collar is a sheet metal fitting used to reconnect a disconnected joint or to bridge a damaged section of flex duct. For a duct that has pulled away from a plenum takeoff collar, the inner liner is slid over the collar with the liner extending past the collar by at least one inch. For a section of duct with a torn inner liner or a kink severe enough to restrict airflow significantly, a splice collar bridges the two cut ends of the duct with the inner liner of each end extending over the collar by at least one inch on each side.^^
The duct strap is a hose clamp or purpose-made band clamp that compresses the inner liner against the collar and creates the mechanical connection. The strap is positioned over the inner liner where it overlaps the collar and tightened until the inner liner is firmly gripping the collar. The strap is the connection. The sealant that follows is the air seal.^^
Mastic sealant is then applied over the inner liner and collar connection, smoothed to cover the full interface between the liner and the collar surface. For accessible connections, mastic provides the durable air seal that tape cannot. UL 181-rated foil tape may supplement the mastic at the butt edge of the outer insulation and vapor barrier, but the structural connection and air seal rely on the strap and mastic respectively, not on tape alone.
This three-component approach, collar plus strap plus mastic, produces a connection that is mechanically secure, air-sealed, and appropriate for the thermal conditions of an unconditioned #State attic. It will not reopen after a hot summer.

The Repair vs. Replace Decision: What System Age and Condition Indicate<h2>
Not every structural duct problem is a repair candidate. The repair vs. replace framework depends on observable conditions.
<list>
Repair is appropriate when the damage is localized: one or two disconnected joints in a system whose overall material condition is sound, a single kinked section on a run that is otherwise well-supported and appropriately tensioned, or a register boot that has pulled away from the ceiling on a system that is in its first half of its service life.
