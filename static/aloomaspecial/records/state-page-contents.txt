Duct Leakage in #State Homes: What Measurement Reveals<h2>
Duct leakage is not uniformly distributed. The most common leak locations in #State residential duct systems are the register boot connections where the duct meets the ceiling or floor surface, the takeoff connections at the main plenum where individual branch runs connect to the trunk, and the mid-run connections where duct sections join at flex fittings or sheet metal collars. These connections were sealed at installation with mastic or tape that has cracked, dried, or separated over years of thermal cycling.
A duct blaster test at a #State property measures total leakage across all these points simultaneously: a calibrated fan pressurizes the system to 25 Pascals and the flow rate required to maintain that pressure is the CFM25 leakage value. We then calculate leakage per 100 square feet of conditioned floor area to normalize the measurement. A #State home with a measured leakage rate of 18 CFM25 per 100 square feet has twice the leakage of the 8 to 10 target for existing homes, which translates directly into the energy and comfort gap the homeowner has been experiencing.
After sealing, the same duct blaster measurement confirms the improvement with a specific number rather than a general assurance. The before-and-after comparison is the documentation that separates a verifiable result from a service performed and assumed to have helped.

What the Leakage Rate Means for Your #State Home's Heating and Cooling Cost<h2>
The relationship between duct leakage and energy cost is mechanical. Every CFM of conditioned air that escapes into an unconditioned space like an attic or crawlspace is air the HVAC system had already expended energy to condition. The system then runs an additional cycle to make up the temperature deficit caused by that loss. In a #State home with a high cooling load in summer, that additional runtime accumulates across every cooling cycle through the hottest months.
Sealing the ducts does not improve the efficiency of the HVAC equipment. It delivers what the equipment already produces to the rooms it was designed to serve rather than to the spaces above the ceiling and below the floor.

Structural Duct Repair in #State<h2>
Physical duct failures in #State homes follow predictable patterns based on duct location and age. Flex duct in attics has a rated service life of 15 to 25 years under normal conditions. Attic heat in #State summers accelerates the degradation of flex duct outer insulation and inner liner material. Flex duct in #State attics that is approaching or past its rated lifespan often shows visible degradation: compressed or torn outer insulation, brittle inner liner material that splits at connection points, and connections that have lost the mastic seal that held them at installation.
Disconnected joints are the most common structural failure we find in #State attic inspections. A joint that has separated allows the full volume of conditioned air intended for the rooms downstream to escape into the attic. The room loses its cooling or heating entirely, and the system works harder because it cannot reach the setpoint even with extended runtime.
We assess the structural condition of #State duct systems as part of every repair and cleaning visit, identify the specific failures present, and provide both spot repair and replacement options when the extent of damage makes both relevant.
