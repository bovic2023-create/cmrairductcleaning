Air Duct Repair and Sealing: Finding What the System Is Losing<h2>
The Department of Energy estimates that homes lose 20 to 30 percent of conditioned air through duct leaks, loose connections, and installation defects. That range means a system moving 1,000 CFM of air from the air handler delivers only 700 to 800 CFM to the living spaces. The rest escapes into attics, wall cavities, crawlspaces, and unconditioned mechanical rooms.
Duct leakage is measured with a duct blaster: a calibrated fan that seals to the air handler and pressurizes the duct system to 25 Pascals above ambient. The flow rate required to maintain that pressure equals the system's leakage in CFM25 (cubic feet per minute at 25 Pascals). Normalized against conditioned floor area, this gives a leakage rate in CFM25 per 100 square feet. Most programs target below 8 to 10 CFM25 per 100 square feet for existing homes. Many homes we test are significantly above that.
Once leakage is measured and documented, we seal it. Accessible joints and connections receive mastic sealant, a thick polymer compound that fills gaps up to 1/4 inch and maintains a durable seal through decades of thermal expansion and contraction. For leaks inside wall and ceiling cavities where physical access would require demolition, aerosolized duct sealant injects pressurized polymer particles into the system. Those particles accumulate at leak openings rather than on duct surfaces, bridging and sealing the gaps from inside. After sealing, a post-treatment duct blaster measurement documents exactly how much the leakage rate improved.

Dryer Vent Cleaning: Code-Compliant Installation and Professional Service<h2>
Dryer vent cleaning removes lint and debris from the exhaust duct run. We also assess whether the installation itself is configured in a way that allows effective cleaning and safe operation.
The International Residential Code Section M1502 specifies requirements for dryer vent installation that many existing homes do not meet: a maximum equivalent duct length of 35 feet (reduced by 5 feet for each 90-degree bend and 2.5 feet for each 45-degree bend), smooth rigid metal duct for the main run, termination to the building exterior with a backdraft damper and no screen, and no termination into attics, crawlspaces, or garages. Screens on exterior termination caps are explicitly prohibited because lint accumulation blocks them rapidly and creates airflow restriction and fire risk.
We clean the full duct run, flush the system with pressurized air, verify airflow at the exterior termination, and note any installation configurations that fall outside code requirements. Knowing what the code requires gives you a specific benchmark to evaluate your existing installation against.

Air Duct Cleaning: Including What Is in the Attic<h2>
Duct cleaning addresses the interior accumulation of debris on duct wall surfaces, the air handler interior, and the register boots. We also inspect the duct system configuration as part of every cleaning visit, because the condition of the ducts themselves affects how long a cleaning result lasts and what problems the system will develop next.
In homes with ductwork in unconditioned attics or crawlspaces, the duct condition is often the primary driver of HVAC performance problems. Flex duct that has sagged, kinked, or partially separated delivers a fraction of the designed airflow to the rooms it serves. Outer insulation that has degraded allows heat exchange between the duct interior and the unconditioned space, reducing cooling capacity of the conditioned air before it reaches the register. We assess these conditions during the cleaning visit and document what we find.

Structural Duct Repair: Reconnection, Support, and Replacement<h2>
Some duct problems are structural rather than sealed: a disconnected joint at a plenum takeoff, a section of flex duct that collapsed when someone walked across it in the attic, a register boot that has pulled away from the ceiling drywall, or a length of flex duct whose inner liner has split. These failures stop airflow to a room or reduce it dramatically.
Structural repairs use specific materials: a splice collar to reconnect a disconnected joint or bridge a damaged section, duct straps to provide the support that prevents future sagging and kinking, and mastic plus UL 181-rated foil tape to seal the reconnection. For systems where the damage is widespread across multiple sections or the material condition of the flex duct has degraded beyond effective repair, we provide a scope for partial or full ductwork replacement alongside the repair option.
